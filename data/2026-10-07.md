**원문 초록 (Abstract)**: Abstract    Background:  Protein engineers often need to choose which amino acid variants to make and test. Computational models rank these variants by their expected performance, such as enzyme activity, but different models recommend different sets. Before committing to a larger experiment, a laboratory needs to know whether a recommended set contains enough variants with the desired experimental performance. This study compared ways to choose measurements that answer that question.  Results:  Methods were evaluated retrospectively using GB1 and adeno-associated virus data, followed by 184 ProteinGym datasets covering 159 proteins. Each ProteinGym model recommended its highest-ranked 10% of variants. A variant counted as a success if it belonged to the experimentally measured top 10% for the protein property being tested. A recommended set was acceptable if more than 20% of its variants were successes. Known experimental outcomes were hidden and revealed sequentially to simulate measuring selected variants. Ten measurement policies were compared. Exact sequential value of information (SeqVOI) had the lowest mean measurement cost: 776.0 measurements, compared with 870.5 for a greedy rule prioritizing variants shared among unresolved model recommendations, and 829.6 for a learned switch between these policies. Costs included initial measurements; runs without a correct decision were charged their full budget. Correct-certification rates were 82.8%, 83.8%, and 82.6%, respectively. The learned switch reduced cost relative to greedy by 5.60%, averaged across proteins, but its one-sided 97.5% lower confidence bound of 4.31% missed the required 5% improvement. Active Testing/LURE recommended sets with success rates closer to the best available model’s than the learned switch at every measured budget. Missing original input-preparation records limit reproducibility.  Conclusions:  Existing model recommendations can guide which protein variants to measure when checking whether a proposed library meets a minimum experimental success rate. Exact SeqVOI had the lowest average cost for this decision in the recorded datasets. Choosing the model with the highest success rate favored different policies. The learned switch did not reduce average cost below that of exact SeqVOI.
